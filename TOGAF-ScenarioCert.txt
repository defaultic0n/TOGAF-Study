Q: The context within which an Enterprise Architecture practitioner must operate
Arlen Group is a holding company with four operating divisions: energy, shipping, property and retail. Each division already has a small architecture team with its own methods and repository. The new Group CIO wants a single Enterprise Architecture Capability that can steer group-wide investments without slowing the divisions down, and asks the Group Chief Architect to start the Preliminary Phase. The divisional CIOs fear that their teams will be absorbed.
What should the Group Chief Architect establish first in the Preliminary Phase?

A: A single central architecture team, by merging the four divisional teams, before any scope or governance is discussed.
Choosing the organizational structure before agreeing scope and governance prejudges the outcome and confirms the divisional CIOs' fears. The TOGAF Standard allows federated models where they fit.
B: A group standard tool for the Architecture Repository, so that all four divisions store their content in one place.
Tool selection is a later step in the Preliminary Phase and depends on the agreed scope, organizational model and framework. Choosing a tool first does not resolve who does what.
C: The scope of the enterprises affected and an organizational model for Enterprise Architecture that defines the roles of the group and divisional teams and their governance relationships.
The Preliminary Phase starts by scoping the enterprise organizations affected and confirming governance and support frameworks, then defines the architecture team and organization. A federated model with clear group and divisional responsibilities addresses both the CIO's goal and the divisions' concerns.
D: A Group Target Business Architecture developed in Phase B for each division, to show the divisions what group-wide alignment means.
Developing Target Architectures is ADM cycle work that needs an established capability, an approved scope and a Statement of Architecture Work. It does not belong in the Preliminary Phase.

Answer: C
Explaination: The Preliminary Phase steps begin with scoping the enterprise organizations impacted and confirming governance and support frameworks, followed by defining and establishing the Enterprise Architecture team and organization, identifying principles, tailoring the framework and implementing tools (TOGAF Standard, ADM, Preliminary Phase; EA Capability and Governance, Establishing an Architecture Capability). In a federated enterprise the key decision is how group and divisional architecture functions relate, which is part of the organizational model for Enterprise Architecture. The scenario's tension between central steering and divisional autonomy is the constraint that makes this the first priority. The distractors jump to structure, tools or architecture content before the scope and model are agreed.


Q: Pemberton Port Authority is ending its Preliminary Phase. The governance office has asked the lead architect for a checklist of the work products the phase should hand over before the first ADM cycle begins. A junior architect has drafted the checklist and included several items that belong to other phases.
Which item on the checklist is an output of the Preliminary Phase?
A: Statement of Architecture Work for the first engagement.
The Statement of Architecture Work is produced and approved in Phase A, in response to a Request for Architecture Work.
B: Architecture Contract with the first delivery team.
Architecture Contracts are agreed during Implementation Governance in Phase G.
C: Organizational Model for Enterprise Architecture.
The Organizational Model for Enterprise Architecture is a Preliminary Phase output, alongside the Tailored Architecture Framework, architecture principles and the initial Architecture Repository.
D: Implementation and Migration Plan.
The Implementation and Migration Plan is drafted in Phase E and finalized in Phase F.
ANSWER: C
Why: The Preliminary Phase prepares the organization for architecture work, and its outputs include the Organizational Model for Enterprise Architecture, the Tailored Architecture Framework (including architecture principles), the initial Architecture Repository and a Request for Architecture Work (TOGAF Standard, ADM, Preliminary Phase). The distractors are real ADM outputs from Phases A, E, F and G. Knowing which outputs belong to which phase helps an architect see whether the capability is ready before an engagement starts.


Q: Hollis Bank is starting an ADM cycle for a new digital banking proposition. The executive committee has already agreed the target operating model at a high level, and the sponsor insists that the new proposition must not inherit the manual workarounds and product silos of today's branch processes. The current landscape includes more than 200 applications from several acquisitions, and the Architecture Board still needs to see what must change to reach the target.
How should the architect structure the architecture development iterations?
A: Use a baseline-first approach: fully document all 200 applications and current processes before considering the target.
The TOGAF Standard describes baseline-first iteration as suitable when the target solution is not clearly understood or agreed, using the baseline assessment to find problem areas. Here the target is already agreed at a high level, and starting from today's processes risks carrying current practice into the target.
B: Use a target-first approach: develop the Target Architecture first, then describe the baseline in enough detail to support gap analysis and migration planning.
The TOGAF Standard describes target-first iteration as suitable when a target state is agreed at a high level and the enterprise wishes to avoid proliferating current business practice into the target model. The target is elaborated in detail and then mapped back to the baseline to identify the change activity.
C: Skip the Baseline Architecture altogether because the target is a new proposition.
Target-first still maps the target back to the baseline. Without any baseline the Architecture Board cannot see what must change, and gap analysis and migration planning lose their foundation.
D: Run a Transition Planning iteration first, covering Phases E and F, to define work packages before any architecture is developed.
Transition Planning iterations depend on the architectures developed in Phases B to D. Planning migration before any architecture exists reverses the dependency.
Answer: B 
Why: The TOGAF Standard describes two styles of architecture development iteration and when each fits (TOGAF Standard, Applying the ADM, Applying Iteration to the ADM). Baseline-first starts from an assessment of the current landscape to identify problems and opportunities, and suits a situation where the target is not clearly understood or agreed. Target-first elaborates the target in detail and then maps it back to the baseline to identify change, and suits a target agreed at a high level where the enterprise wants to avoid carrying current practice into the new model. The decisive facts are the agreed high-level operating model and the sponsor's demand not to inherit today's workarounds, while the Board's need to see the changes means the baseline is still described as far as gap analysis requires. A common trap is to pick baseline-first simply because the current estate is large; the TOGAF criterion is how well the target is understood and agreed.

Q: Tamsin Insurance's board has approved enterprise principles, including "Customer Data Belongs to the Customer", which commits the company to let customers see and export all data held about them. In the Preliminary Phase, the IT architecture team drafts a data principle stating that customer data is internal to the company and may be released only to regulators. The draft is submitted to the Architecture Board for approval.
What should the Architecture Board require?
A: Approve the draft, because architecture principles take precedence over enterprise principles for IT decisions.
The TOGAF Standard places architecture principles beneath enterprise principles. They elaborate and apply enterprise principles; they do not override them.
B: Grant the IT team a dispensation from the enterprise principle so that both can stand.
Dispensations are time-limited exceptions for specific solutions. They are not a way to create a standing architecture principle that contradicts an enterprise principle.
C: Keep both principles and let each project choose which one to follow.
Two contradictory principles provide no guidance and invite inconsistent decisions, failing the consistency criterion for good principles.
D: Revise the draft so that it is consistent with and derived from the enterprise principle, with a rationale that refers to it.
Architecture principles should be informed by, and consistent with, enterprise principles. Linking the rationale to the higher principle shows that relationship.
Answer: D
Why: The TOGAF Standard describes a hierarchy in which enterprise principles inform architecture principles, which in turn guide solution decisions; architecture principles must be consistent with the enterprise principles they elaborate (TOGAF Standard, ADM Techniques, Architecture Principles). The scenario's draft directly contradicts a board-approved enterprise principle, so it cannot be approved as written. The distractors misuse dispensations, invert the hierarchy, or accept contradictory guidance.

Q: How to apply the stakeholder management technique
At Benham Telecom, the architect's stakeholder map for a network billing engagement lists each stakeholder group and its power/interest quadrant, and nothing else. When the team starts Phase B, nobody can say which catalogs, matrices or diagrams each group expects, and two weeks are lost producing artifacts no one asked for.
What should the architect add to the stakeholder map?
A: A RACI chart assigning each stakeholder to the project's delivery tasks.
A RACI chart is a project management tool for tasks. It does not tell the architecture team what each stakeholder needs to see.
B: Each stakeholder's job grade and reporting line, to show their seniority.
Seniority may inform power, but it does not describe concerns or deliverables, which is what the team was missing.
C: A list of the Architecture Contracts each stakeholder will sign.
Architecture Contracts are Phase G agreements. They do not guide what architecture content to produce in Phases B to D.
D: For each stakeholder group, its key concerns and the catalogs, matrices and diagrams that address them.
The stakeholder map in the TOGAF Standard records, for each stakeholder group, its involvement and key concerns, its class and the artifacts relevant to it, so that the map drives the content produced.
Answer: D
Why: The stakeholder management technique ends by tailoring engagement deliverables: the stakeholder map records each group's concerns, class and the relevant catalogs, matrices and diagrams (TOGAF Standard, ADM Techniques, Stakeholder Management). A map with only quadrants tells the team how much to engage each group but not what to produce. The deciding fact is the wasted work on unrequested artifacts. The distractors add project or contractual information that does not connect stakeholders to architecture content.


Q: Marlow Credit Union completed Phase A of a lending platform engagement with an approved Statement of Architecture Work, a stakeholder map and a Communications Plan. Midway through Phase B, the Chief Operating Officer who championed the work leaves and is replaced by an executive from a competitor who has publicly favoured buying a packaged lending suite rather than the planned integration approach.
What should the architect do next?
A: Revisit the stakeholder analysis for the new COO, update the stakeholder map and Communications Plan, and engage her early on her concerns and the options being considered.
Stakeholder management is continuous. A new high-power stakeholder with different views must be analyzed and engaged before the architecture progresses further.
B: Continue with the existing Communications Plan, because it was approved in Phase A.
Approval does not freeze the plan. Ignoring a change in a key stakeholder's position risks the architecture being rejected later.
C: Restart the engagement from the Preliminary Phase to re-establish the Architecture Capability.
The Architecture Capability is unaffected by one executive change. Restarting the Preliminary Phase is out of proportion to the problem.
D: Raise a change request in Phase H to record the new COO's preference for a packaged suite.
Phase H manages change to an implemented architecture baseline. A new stakeholder's preference during Phase B is a stakeholder engagement matter, and any resulting requirement goes through Requirements Management.
Answer: A
Why: The TOGAF Standard treats stakeholder analysis as starting in Phase A and being revisited throughout the ADM as stakeholders and their positions change (TOGAF Standard, ADM Techniques, Stakeholder Management). A replacement COO is a high-power stakeholder whose position has moved, so the map and the Communications Plan must be updated and engagement planned to address her concerns. The distractors either treat the plan as fixed or reach for heavyweight mechanisms such as restarting the capability or Phase H.

Q: Kinross Airports is in Phase D of an architecture for a new passenger check-in platform. The operations director's main concern is what happens to check-in if one of the two data centres fails. The architect has produced structural diagrams of the platform's components, but none of the viewpoints the team normally uses shows failure behaviour or recovery. The Architecture Repository holds a library of viewpoints that other teams have defined.
What should the architect do?
A: Record the concern as out of scope, because the standard viewpoints the team uses do not cover it.
Concerns of key stakeholders must be addressed. A gap in the team's usual viewpoints is a reason to find or define one, not to drop the concern.
B :Add availability notes to every existing structural diagram and send the full set to the operations director.
A structural viewpoint is not designed to show failure and recovery behaviour. Annotating many diagrams leaves the director to assemble the answer.
C: Send the operations director the Technology Standards catalog showing the approved data centre products.
The catalog lists approved technology. It does not show how check-in behaves when a data centre fails.
D: Look for a suitable viewpoint in the repository's viewpoint library and, if none fits, define one for resilience and recovery, produce the view, and add the viewpoint to the library for reuse.
Viewpoints are reusable and stored in the Architecture Repository. When none addresses a concern, the architect defines one, which then serves future engagements.
Answer: D
Why: The TOGAF Standard holds that each view must conform to a viewpoint that frames the concerns of its stakeholders, and that viewpoints can be reused from a library or newly defined when the concerns require it (TOGAF Standard, Architecture Content, Architectural Artifacts). The deciding fact is that the team's usual viewpoints are structural, while the director's concern is behaviour under failure. Defining or reusing an appropriate viewpoint, and storing it in the repository, addresses the concern and builds the capability. The distractors drop the concern, overload the stakeholder, or answer a different question.


Q: Aldermoor Chemicals is in Phase A of a global procurement architecture. Stakeholders include plant managers in nine countries, the group procurement board, IT application owners and an external auditor. The architect is writing the Communications Plan and a colleague suggests keeping it simple.
What should the Communications Plan specify?
A: A single monthly newsletter sent to every stakeholder so that all groups receive the same message.
Different stakeholder groups have different concerns and needs. One message for all ignores the stakeholder analysis that the plan is built on.
B: Communication with the Key Players only, since other groups cannot affect the outcome.
Keep Satisfied, Keep Informed and even Minimal Effort stakeholders still need planned treatment. The external auditor and plant managers would be left out.
C: For each stakeholder group, the information they need, the communication mechanisms to use, and the timing of communications aligned to engagement milestones.
A Communications Plan identifies stakeholders, their communication needs, mechanisms such as meetings, newsletters and repositories, and a timetable tied to the engagement.
D: That all architecture content will be published in the Architecture Repository, where stakeholders can find what they need.
The repository is a useful mechanism, but publishing alone is not planned communication. Stakeholders who do not know what to look for will miss what matters to them.
Answer: C
Why: The Communications Plan, an output of Phase A, sets out how the architecture team will communicate with stakeholders: who receives what, through which mechanisms and when (TOGAF Standard, ADM, Phase A; ADM Techniques, Stakeholder Management). It is built from the stakeholder analysis, so it differs by stakeholder group. The distractors simplify the plan in ways that ignore the classification of stakeholders.

Q: How to implement the Architecture Vision phase including its applicable techniques
Larchfield University has identified the main risks of moving student records to a new platform, assessed their initial level, and assigned a mitigation approach to each. The sponsor wants to be sure that the cost and effort of mitigation are agreed as part of the engagement rather than discovered later.
Where should the architect place the risks and mitigation activities in Phase A?
A: Include them in the Statement of Architecture Work so they are approved with the engagement scope and plan
Phase A identifies business transformation risks and mitigation activities and includes them in the Statement of Architecture Work, so the sponsor approves them as part of the engagement.
B: Record them only in the Compliance Assessments that the Architecture Board performs in Phase G
Compliance Assessments review implementation projects in Phase G. They are too late to agree the cost of mitigating risks identified in Phase A.
C: Hold them back until Phase F, when the Implementation and Migration Plan is costed
Phase F refines and sequences mitigation within the plan, but the risks are first identified and agreed in Phase A.
D: Add them to the architecture principles so all future projects must mitigate them
Principles are general rules that guide decisions. Engagement-specific risks and their mitigation do not belong there.
Answer: A
Why: Phase A in the TOGAF Standard includes the step 'Identify the Business Transformation Risks and Mitigation Activities'. The architect identifies the risks associated with the Architecture Vision, assesses their initial level, assigns a mitigation strategy, and includes these considerations in the Statement of Architecture Work. That way the sponsor approves the mitigation effort together with scope and schedule, which is the scenario's stated need. The distractors use real TOGAF outputs that are either too late or the wrong kind of document.


Q: Calder Freight's Statement of Architecture Work was approved for a European parcel network. Midway through Phase B, the sponsor asks the team to add the North American subsidiary, which has different regulations, systems and stakeholders. The approved document includes a change of scope procedure.
What should the architect do?
A: Add North America to the Phase B work and keep the existing plan and acceptance criteria
Absorbing a major scope change without assessment leaves schedule, deliverables and acceptance criteria inconsistent with the work being done.
B: Raise a change request through Architecture Change Management in Phase H
Phase H manages change to implemented architectures through the change management process. A scope change to an engagement in progress follows that engagement's own procedure.
C: Restart the Preliminary Phase to redefine the organization's Architecture Capability
The Architecture Capability is not in question. The change concerns the scope of one engagement.
D: Apply the change of scope procedure: assess the impact on scope, stakeholders, plan and deliverables, revisit the Architecture Vision as needed, and obtain approval of an updated Statement of Architecture Work
The change of scope procedure in the Statement of Architecture Work exists for this case. A large addition with new stakeholders and regulations calls for an updated vision and a re-approved agreement.
Answer: D
Why: The TOGAF Standard includes specific change of scope procedures in the Statement of Architecture Work so that changes during the engagement are handled in an agreed way. Adding a subsidiary with its own regulations, systems and stakeholders changes scope, stakeholders and possibly the vision, so the architect assesses the impact, revisits Phase A outputs as needed, and gets the updated Statement of Architecture Work approved before continuing. The ADM's iterative nature supports returning to Phase A when scope changes substantially. The distractors either ignore the procedure, use Phase H change management for a scope change, or reset the whole Architecture Capability.

The other options are real TOGAF activities placed in the wrong phase or used for a different purpose.


Q: Ellsworth Airports is developing a business scenario in Phase A for automated baggage tracking. The first draft says the objective is 'improve baggage handling' and lists only the baggage system as an actor. The business line managers have not been involved yet.
Which three changes should the architect make to the business scenario? Each correct answer presents part of the solution.
A: Restate the objectives so they are specific, measurable, actionable, realistic and time-bound
The business scenario technique requires SMART objectives, so the vague 'improve baggage handling' must be made measurable and time-bound.
B: Identify the human actors, such as handlers and passengers, as well as the computer actors, and define their roles and responsibilities
A business scenario names both human and computer actors and their roles, which reveals requirements a single system actor hides.
C: Involve business line management and the people who will use the solution in developing and reviewing the scenario
Business scenarios are developed with business users and line management, not by architects alone, so the requirements reflect the real business need.
D: Select the specific baggage tracking products in the scenario so that the requirements are realistic
Business scenarios describe the business problem and requirements, not product choices. Products are selected much later, during implementation planning.
E: Replace the Statement of Architecture Work with the finished business scenario
A business scenario is a technique for discovering requirements and supporting the vision. It does not replace the Statement of Architecture Work.
Answer: A, B, C
Why: The business scenario technique, which Phase A refers to and the TOGAF Series Guide on Business Scenarios describes, identifies the problem, the business and technical environment, the objectives, the human and computer actors, and their roles and responsibilities, and then checks fitness for purpose and refines the scenario. Objectives are expected to be SMART, and the scenario is developed in collaboration with business users and line management. The draft in the scenario fails on all three counts. The distractors introduce product selection too early or confuse the technique with the Phase A agreement document.

B: A data migration diagram
The data migration diagram shows the flow of data from baseline sources to target, including the transformations applied, which is what the migration team needs.
C: An application/technology matrix
This Phase D matrix maps applications to technology components. It does not describe data migration.
D: A business footprint diagram
A Business Footprint diagram is a Business Architecture artifact that links business goals, organization units, functions and services to the technical components that deliver them. It is not a migration view.

It helps plan cleansing, quality checks and the order of migration. The scenario asks for exactly that baseline-to-target view. The distractors are real artifacts that relate data, applications or goals to something else.


Q: Kestrel Logistics is completing gap analysis in Phase C. The Application Architecture gap matrix lists Baseline building blocks as rows and Target building blocks as columns, with a final 'New' row on the Baseline axis and a final 'Eliminated' column on the Target axis. The baseline 'Fax Order Intake' application has no matching Target column and is marked in the Eliminated column. The 'Partner API Gateway' appears only in the New row.
Which two conclusions should the architect draw from the matrix? Each correct answer presents part of the solution.
A: Fax Order Intake must be checked to confirm whether it was intentionally eliminated or accidentally omitted from the Target Architecture
A baseline building block missing from the target is a gap that must be reviewed. If its function is still needed, the omission must be corrected in the target.
B: The Partner API Gateway is a gap that must be filled by developing or procuring the building block
Items in the New row are target building blocks with no baseline counterpart. They represent capability that must be built or bought.
C: Every cell marked 'Included' means the building block must be rebuilt for the target
'Included' means the building block carries forward from baseline to target. It is not a gap and does not imply rebuilding.
D: Fax Order Intake should be deleted from the Architecture Repository now that the matrix shows it as eliminated
The repository keeps baseline descriptions; elimination is a planned change carried into the roadmap, not an immediate deletion of the record.
E: The gaps should be left unrecorded until Phase E consolidates them
Each of Phases B, C and D performs and records its own gap analysis. Phase E consolidates the gaps that those phases have already identified.
Answer: A, B
Why: The Gap Analysis technique (ADM Techniques, Chapter 5) uses a matrix with Baseline building blocks on the vertical axis and Target building blocks on the horizontal axis, a final row labeled New and a final column labeled Eliminated. A new item in the target is a gap to be filled by development or procurement. An item eliminated from the baseline must be checked: if the elimination was deliberate it is recorded as such, and if it was accidental the target must be corrected. The distractors misread 'Included', treat the repository as a list of current systems only, or push gap recording to Phase E.

Q: How to implement the ADM Phases E, F, and G to implement an architecture, together with the applicable techniques
Marrick County is in Phase E of an architecture that links its social-services case system with three partner agencies. Today the agencies email spreadsheets to each other. The Target Architecture requires that case records sent by one agency are processed automatically by the receiving system using a shared data model, without staff re-keying them. Stakeholders disagree on how much integration is really needed.
How should the architect express and agree the interoperability needed for each exchange?
A: Use the Interoperability Requirements technique to state the required degree of interoperability for each information exchange, such as seamless sharing of data, and record it in an interoperability matrix agreed with the stakeholders.
The technique defines degrees of interoperability, from unstructured data exchange through structured exchange and seamless sharing of data to seamless sharing of information, so each exchange can be stated and agreed precisely.
B: Run a Business Transformation Readiness Assessment for each agency and use the readiness scores as the interoperability requirements.
A readiness assessment evaluates an organisation's ability to undergo change. It does not specify how systems must exchange data.
C: Add the partner agencies to the power/interest grid and let the agency with the highest power decide the integration level.
Stakeholder mapping decides how to engage stakeholders. It is not a method for defining interoperability, and letting one stakeholder decide skips the requirements agreement.
D: Leave the integration level for each implementation project to decide in Phase G and capture the decision in its Architecture Contract.
Interoperability requirements are consolidated and reconciled in Phase E so that work packages and Transition Architectures can be planned. Deferring them to Phase G leaves the migration plan without a basis.

Why: The TOGAF Standard (ADM Techniques, Interoperability Requirements) describes how to define interoperability as degrees, ranging from unstructured data exchange (Degree 1), structured data exchange (Degree 2), seamless sharing of data (Degree 3) to seamless sharing of information (Degree 4). Phase E includes a step to consolidate and reconcile interoperability requirements, because conflicting expectations here change which work packages are needed. The distractors are real techniques used for the wrong purpose or at the wrong time.
